When is pavement too hot for dog paws?

The short answer: far earlier in the day, and far later into the evening, than the air temperature suggests.

The gap nobody feels

You are wearing shoes. Your dog is not, and the surface they walk on is not the air — it is a black solar collector that has been charging all afternoon.

On a 75°F day that feels genuinely pleasant, clear-sky asphalt runs around 125°F. That is roughly a 50-degree gap between what the forecast tells you and what their pads touch. Concrete sidewalks run considerably cooler, but "cooler" is not the same as "safe."

What the numbers mean for paws

  • Below 110°F — generally fine for normal walk lengths.
  • 110–125°F — uncomfortable, and risky over distance. Keep it short, stick to grass and shade, walk early.
  • 125°F and above — skin damage has been reported from about a minute of contact at this temperature. This is potty-break-on-the-grass territory, not walk territory.

Notice where 125°F lands on the chart: a 75°F sunny afternoon. Most people would not think twice about that forecast.

The 7-second test beats every estimate — including ours

Press the back of your hand flat against the pavement and hold it for seven seconds. If you have to lift it early, it is too hot for their pads.

Use the back of your hand rather than your palm; palms are calloused and will happily lie to you. Test the surface you are actually about to walk on — a shaded driveway and the sunlit road ten feet away can differ by 30 degrees.

Evenings are hotter than they feel

Waiting for sunset is the standard advice, and it is only half right. Pavement has thermal mass: it keeps releasing the day's heat for hours after the sun is gone.

We checked this against 16 Arizona DOT roadside sensors at 11:13 PM local, just under four hours after sunset. The road was warmer than the air at every single station — on average +6.8°F, and up to +10.1°F.

That is the window when people are told to walk their dogs. If you plan the evening walk off the air temperature alone, you are consistently underestimating the ground by five to ten degrees — in the exact hours when you were trying to be careful.

If the pads are already burned

Signs to look for: limping or refusing to walk, licking or chewing at the feet, pads that look darker than usual, missing patches of pad, or visible blisters.

  • Get them off the hot surface — carry them if you can.
  • Cool the feet with cool (not ice) water.
  • Keep the area clean and stop them licking it.
  • Call your vet. Burned pads can get infected, and blistering or missing pad tissue needs to be seen.

This page is general information for planning walks, not veterinary advice.

Where these numbers come from

The estimates are produced by the same model that runs inside PawWeather, driven by National Weather Service forecasts (api.weather.gov) plus sun angle, cloud cover and surface type.

We validate it against Arizona DOT's roadside sensor network rather than trusting the formula. Two things came out of that. In clear-sky daylight our estimate reads above what the highway sensors report — embedded sensors in a road are cooled by wind and passing traffic, and a residential sidewalk is neither, so we left the model on the cautious side of that gap. And the night check above is what made us add thermal lag: modelling evenings as "pavement equals air" was wrong by an average of 6.8°F, and it was wrong in the direction that matters.
